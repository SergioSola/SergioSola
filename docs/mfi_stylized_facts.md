# Stylized Facts: Monetary-Fiscal Interactions

## 1. Rising Public Debt in Advanced Economies

- Average debt-to-GDP in advanced economies rose from ~70% in 2007 to ~120% by 2023
  (IMF Fiscal Monitor).
- The COVID-19 pandemic added 15–30 percentage points to debt ratios in a single year.
- Japan (>250%), Italy (~140%), and the US (~120%) are prominent high-debt cases.

## 2. Low Interest Rates and the "r − g" Dynamic

- For much of 2010–2021, the interest-growth differential (r − g) was negative in
  most advanced economies, supporting debt sustainability despite high ratios.
- Post-2022, rising rates have turned (r − g) positive in several countries,
  reigniting fiscal sustainability concerns.
- The key question: was the low-rate era a structural shift or a temporary anomaly?

## 3. Central Bank Balance Sheets and Fiscal Entanglement

- Quantitative easing blurred the boundary between monetary and fiscal policy:
  central banks hold 20–40% of outstanding government debt in major economies.
- Remittances from central banks to treasuries created a fiscal windfall during
  low-rate periods; rising rates have reversed this, generating central bank losses.
- De facto shortening of government debt maturity through QE increases rollover risk.

## 4. Sovereign Risk Premia and Fiscal Credibility

- Sovereign spreads respond nonlinearly to debt: near-zero for low-debt countries,
  rising steeply as debt approaches perceived limits (see Italian BTP-Bund spread).
- Market discipline is episodic: long periods of complacency followed by sudden
  repricing (e.g., Euro crisis 2010–2012, UK gilt crisis 2022).
- Inflation expectations embed fiscal credibility: breakeven inflation rates
  diverge across countries with different fiscal positions.

## 5. The Sovereign-Bank Nexus

- Banks in many countries hold 10–25% of domestic sovereign debt (home bias).
- Sovereign downgrades mechanically tighten bank capital constraints.
- During the Euro crisis, the "doom loop" between sovereign and bank risk was
  a primary amplification mechanism.
- Post-crisis regulation (Basel III) has not fully addressed the sovereign
  exposure concentration.

## 6. Corporate Borrowing Costs and the Sovereign Floor

- Corporate bond yields are bounded below by the sovereign yield curve:
  the "sovereign ceiling" (or floor) transmits sovereign stress to private credit.
- In stressed episodes, corporate spreads widen even for firms with no direct
  government exposure, via the banking channel and general risk aversion.
- Investment and hiring decisions are sensitive to the cost of credit,
  creating a real-economy channel for fiscal-monetary tensions.

## 7. Exchange Rate and Capital Flow Dynamics

- Currencies of high-debt countries tend to depreciate during fiscal stress episodes,
  amplifying the debt burden for foreign-currency-denominated obligations.
- Foreign investor share of sovereign debt varies widely: ~30% for the US,
  ~25% for Italy, ~50% for some emerging markets.
- Capital flow reversals during fiscal stress episodes ("sudden stops") can
  trigger balance-of-payments crises even in advanced economies (e.g., UK 2022).

## 8. Policy Regime Changes

- Historical episodes of fiscal dominance: US 1940s (war finance with yield curve
  control), Latin America 1980s, potentially Euro periphery 2010–2012.
- The transition from monetary dominance to fiscal dominance is typically gradual
  and difficult to detect in real time.
- Market expectations of regime change can be self-fulfilling: if investors expect
  fiscal dominance, they demand higher premia, worsening the fiscal position.
